## The Data Behind the Decision

The clearest verified evidence on communication and no-shows in rheumatology comes from an academic clinic's patient portal rollout. Researchers at Sunnybrook Health Sciences Centre tracked 274 surveyed patients and recorded 110 no-shows over six months, with rates ranging from 2.5% to 6.8% depending on the rheumatologist. Interviews with 33 no-show patients found 85% of missed visits traced to forgetting the appointment, not knowing about it, or thinking it was on a different date: 45.5% simply forgot or were unaware, and 21.2% had the wrong date in mind. Just 44% of surveyed patients were enrolled in the clinic's patient portal at baseline, though enrollment among patients who did attend climbed from 47% toward roughly 70% by the later survey. An early improvement after adding email reminders through the portal was not sustained in the following two months (Mendel and Chow, *BMJ Quality Improvement Reports*, 2017).

A second data point suggests the channel matters as much as the reminder itself. A county hospital rheumatology clinic serving a multilingual population at UCSF reported an average no-show rate of 24% in 2014. After building a multilingual team that made live reminder phone calls in English, Spanish, Cantonese, and Mandarin, the median rate fell to 11.6% over four months in early 2015, across 331 appointments (Beach et al., American College of Rheumatology Annual Meeting abstract, 2015). Automated reminders alone, in the Sunnybrook study, did not hold up past the first month. A live, two-way conversation did more, in a different clinic serving a different population, which is the same pattern a communications platform is built to exploit: reach the patient on a channel they will actually respond to, not just one that sends a message.

## What does patient communications software actually need to cover in a rheumatology practice?

At minimum, six message types across a visit that often spans a referral, an infusion schedule, and ongoing lab monitoring: new-consult intake and triage from a referring primary care physician flagging elevated inflammatory markers, a positive ANA or RF, or a suspected inflammatory arthritis; appointment reminders for both consult and infusion or injection visits; pre-infusion instructions such as premedication, confirming recent labs are on file, and current prior authorization status; post-infusion and post-visit follow-up that distinguishes a routine check-in from an infusion reaction or a sign of infection in an immunosuppressed patient; DMARD and biologic lab-monitoring recall, so a required CBC or liver panel does not quietly lapse and force a dose to be held; and prior authorization and insurance tracking, since most biologics require authorization before every fill or infusion. Most practices run each of these through a different tool or a staff member's personal tracking system. No single system today knows a patient is three days post-infusion, due for a monitoring lab next month, and still waiting on a reauthorization for the next dose.

Communications software is supposed to put every message type into one record per patient, reachable on whatever channel that patient actually answers. It only earns the name if it treats a reported post-infusion symptom differently from a routine reminder, because the two require different urgency and different staff, not the same template.

## Why does a reminder-only or single-channel tool fail rheumatology practices the most?

Most communications platforms default to whichever channel was cheapest to build, usually a one-way text or an automated call, and stop at the next-visit reminder. That default is tolerable for confirming a routine follow-up. It fails on the message types where rheumatology's real risk sits: a post-infusion message that could be a routine question or could be a patient on a biologic reporting a fever, and a monitoring lab interval that has no visit attached to trigger a reminder at all.

A reminder-only tool also cannot answer back. A patient asking whether a premedication instruction applies before their next infusion, or reporting a reaction three days after one, gets silence unless a staff member happens to see the message in time. A prior authorization that stalls silently in a payer queue can push a scheduled infusion back weeks before anyone at the practice notices, and a missed dose interval can mean restarting a loading schedule. Those are exactly the gaps a two-way, record-aware system is built to close.

## How to evaluate rheumatology patient communications software before you buy: 5 steps

1. Confirm the system reads and writes to your practice management or EHR system, and that it can track a single patient record across referral, infusion schedule, and lab monitoring, not just the next appointment.
2. Check that DMARD and biologic lab-monitoring recall runs on its own tracked interval, independent of whether a visit is already on the calendar.
3. Verify every channel is two-way, so a patient reporting a post-infusion symptom or asking about a premedication instruction reaches a response, not a dead end.
4. Ask how the platform distinguishes a routine message from an urgent one, and whether a reported infection sign or infusion reaction routes to clinical staff the same day.
5. Confirm prior authorization and insurance tracking is built in, with a visible status so a stalled authorization surfaces before it delays a scheduled infusion.

## What Should Still Go to a Human?

Any clinical judgment call: interpreting a lab result, deciding whether to hold or resume a DMARD or biologic, and any post-infusion or post-visit symptom that sounds urgent, a fever, a severe injection-site reaction, or a sign of infection in a patient on immunosuppressive therapy, which should route to clinical staff the same day rather than sit in a queue. Software can confirm a referral was received, track whether a monitoring interval is due, and flag a reported symptom fast; the rheumatologist still makes the clinical call. Prior authorization appeals and billing disputes go to your billing team.
