# Neurology Patient Communications Software: What to Look for Before You Buy

## The Data Behind the Decision

The clearest evidence on appointment risk in neurology specifically comes from a five-year retrospective review of 71,178 appointments across neurology clinics at the University of Arkansas for Medical Sciences. The study found 11,166 no-shows, a 16% rate overall, and identified specific risk factors: younger patients, women, Black and African American patients, patients covered by Medicare or Medicaid, longer lead time between scheduling and the visit, and resident and stroke clinics carried higher odds of a no-show than general neurology ([Elkhider et al., *Healthcare*, 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9025597/)). The authors' own recommendation was better communication, more flexible scheduling for the identified high-risk groups, and telemedicine, not a single blanket reminder sent to every patient on the same schedule.

Call volume tells the other half of the story. A community pediatric neurology clinic at Texas Children's Hospital tracked its average monthly after-visit call volume at a baseline of 293 calls. Using a structured improvement project, including assigning each physician a dedicated nurse to share the call workload and revising the clinic's phone tree, the practice cut that volume 30%, to 203 calls a month, and held the reduction for more than a year. Portal message volume did not drop to compensate. It held steady at roughly 359 messages a month across the same period ([Bala, *Neurology: Clinical Practice*, 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10955459)). Read together, the two studies point at the same gap from different directions: neurology generates a heavy, recurring volume of between-visit contact, much of it driven by medication questions, symptom concerns, and monitoring intervals rather than the next scheduled appointment, and the patients most likely to miss that next appointment are often the same ones a generic reminder system is least equipped to reach.

Neurology also carries message types most specialties do not. A missed seizure medication dose, a new or worsening symptom, or an unscheduled EEG or MRI order is not the same category of problem as a routine reminder, and treating it that way is how the gap above forms in the first place.

## What does patient communications software actually need to cover in a neurology practice?

At minimum, five message types: medication and infusion adherence reminders for seizure, MS, and movement-disorder regimens running on their own clocks, between-visit symptom triage that separates a routine question from a red-flag report, follow-up on EEG, EMG, and imaging orders a patient never scheduled, referral coordination in from primary care, the emergency department, and stroke teams, and recall for long-interval monitoring visits where the gap between appointments can run months. Most practices run each of these through a different tool: a nurse line for symptom calls, a portal message for imaging results, a fax or phone call for a referral. No single tool knows a patient is overdue for an MS infusion and also has an unscheduled EEG sitting on the same chart.

Communications software is supposed to put every message type into one record per patient, reachable on whatever channel that patient or caregiver answers. It only earns the name if it treats a reported new symptom differently than a routine medication reminder, because both studies above point to the same thing: the volume is real, and a chunk of it is clinically urgent.

## Why does a reminder-only or single-channel tool fail neurology practices the most?

Most communications platforms default to whichever channel was cheapest to build, usually a one-way text or an automated call, and stop at the next scheduled visit. That default is tolerable for confirming a routine follow-up. It fails on the message types neurology generates most: a caregiver calling to report a new seizure, a patient asking whether a medication side effect is expected, or a referral from the emergency department that needs same-week scheduling, not the next open slot three months out.

A reminder-only tool also cannot answer back or triage. The UAMS study found resident and stroke clinics carried higher no-show odds than general neurology, exactly the populations most likely to generate an urgent, time-sensitive message in between visits. A channel that cannot route "new seizure since last visit" differently than "please confirm your Tuesday appointment" pushes the real question back to a phone call anyway, just later, and often after the call volume has already built up the way it did at the Texas Children's clinic before that practice restructured its workflow.

## How to evaluate neurology patient communications software before you buy: 5 steps

1. Confirm the system reads and writes to your EHR, Epic, athenahealth, eClinicalWorks, NextGen, or Cerner among them, rather than working from a stale export.
2. Check that medication and infusion adherence reminders, symptom triage, and long-interval recall each run on their own schedule inside one patient record, not a single shared reminder cadence.
3. Verify every channel is two-way and that a reported symptom gets flagged and routed, not just logged.
4. Ask how the platform follows up with a patient or caregiver who ignores the first message, and whether that follow-up happens automatically or depends on staff remembering to check.
5. Confirm consent, opt-in, and HIPAA handling across phone, text, and portal, and how an inbound referral from primary care or the ED gets documented in the same thread instead of a separate fax or phone call nobody logs.

## What Should Still Go to a Human?

Any new or worsening neurological symptom, a reported seizure, a stroke warning sign, a significant medication reaction, should route to clinical staff the same day, not sit in a queue waiting for a scheduled check. Deciding on a medication change, interpreting an EEG or imaging result, and any clinical judgment call stay with the physician. Software can notify a patient that a monitoring visit is due or an order has been placed and get them scheduled fast; it should never be the one deciding what a symptom means. Prior-authorization appeals and billing disputes go to your front-office or billing team.
