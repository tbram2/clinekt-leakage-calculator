What does an after-hours answering service cost?

Pricing for a traditional medical answering service depends on how the vendor bills you. Most use one of four models: per call, per minute, a flat monthly bundle, or a per-provider rate.

According to a 2026 pricing guide from Anytime Telecare, per-minute plans run $1.75 to $2.25 a minute, and per-call plans run $0.96 to $1.84 a call. A practice with five to ten providers handling 200 to 500 after-hours calls a month typically budgets $400 to $1,500 a month. Full 24/7 multi-provider coverage, the kind an orthopedic group with a rotating surgeon call schedule needs, runs $1,000 to $3,000 or more.

The advertised rate is rarely the final bill. The same guide notes that setup fees, holiday and after-hours differentials, bilingual surcharges, and HIPAA compliance riders inflate the sticker price by 25 to 50 percent. A practice quoted $800 a month can end up paying $1,000 to $1,200 once those charges land.

What does AI after-hours coverage cost instead?

AI-based after-hours coverage sits at a different price point, and the gap is wide. A June 2026 cost comparison from Gabbyville puts basic AI call handling as low as $25 a month, against $299 to $999 a month for a bundled human-staffed service with HIPAA compliance and 24/7 coverage built in.

That entry-level AI pricing describes simple call routing, not a full patient activation agent that can check the schedule, verify context, and book a real appointment. AI-based coverage still costs meaningfully less than a human answering service at every pricing tier, and the gap grows as call volume rises, because AI pricing does not scale per minute the way legacy plans do.

Why do practices still pay for a human answering service?

Two reasons come up constantly in ortho and oral surgery: liability and complexity. A surgeon on call for post-op complications wants a real person triaging genuine emergencies. And legacy answering services were built before software could reliably sort "I have a question about my brace" from "I am having a surgical emergency."

Neither reason carries the weight it used to. Clinekt's Inbound agent routes anything that looks like a true emergency to a human immediately, and handles the rest of the call volume, which is most of it, without a person on the line.

What can AI handle after hours, and what still needs a human?

An AI agent built for after-hours coverage can:

  • Answer the call within seconds instead of routing to voicemail.
  • Ask structured triage questions to sort routine calls from urgent ones.
  • Book, reschedule, or confirm an appointment against the live schedule.
  • Answer common post-op and pre-op questions using practice-specific protocols.
  • Escalate anything resembling a genuine emergency directly to the on-call surgeon or a live line.
  • Log every call and outcome so the front desk sees a complete record the next morning instead of a stack of paper messages.

A human still needs to handle any call describing symptoms consistent with a surgical emergency, any call from a referring physician's office that needs a judgment call, and any call where de-escalating a distressed patient matters more than collecting data. A well-built after-hours agent is designed to recognize those calls and get a person on the line fast, not to replace a surgeon's judgment.

How do you evaluate whether to switch?

  1. Pull your last three months of after-hours call volume from your current answering service invoice, not an estimate.
  2. Break that volume into categories: appointment requests, post-op questions, prescription questions, and true emergencies.
  3. Ask your current vendor for the fully loaded monthly cost, including every surcharge, not the advertised rate.
  4. Estimate how many of those calls could have ended in a booked or confirmed appointment instead of a callback the next morning.
  5. Compare that lost-booking number against the price gap between your current service and an AI-based alternative.
  6. Pilot the switch on one call type first, such as routine post-op questions or appointment requests, before moving emergency triage over.
