## The Data Behind the Decision

The clearest evidence that parents want electronic communication, not just tolerate it, comes from a 2024 scoping review that searched PubMed, CINAHL, Embase, and Web of Science for studies on caregiver experience with electronic communication published between 2013 and 2023. Of the 23 articles that met inclusion criteria, parents reported positive experiences with, and a desire for, e-communication methods in 19 of them, and most of those studies centered on patient-portal messaging specifically (Gamper et al., <a href="https://doi.org/10.2196/60352" target="_blank" rel="noopener"><em>JMIR Pediatrics and Parenting</em>, 2024</a>). The same review found a gap worth noting: no study in its sample intentionally tested how to improve the quality of that communication, only whether parents wanted it.

Channel preference is more specific than "parents like texting." A 2018 survey distributed to 463 parents of 10-to-17-year-olds across practice-based research networks in South Carolina and Oklahoma asked how they wanted their teen's physician to send vaccine reminders: 70% chose text, 19% chose a phone call, 10% chose email, and 1% chose mail (Roberts et al., <a href="https://doi.org/10.1016/j.vaccine.2018.03.080" target="_blank" rel="noopener"><em>Vaccine</em>, 2018</a>). That is a strong preference, not a unanimous one, which is exactly why a communications platform needs more than one channel and needs to know which channel a given family actually answers.

## What does patient communications software actually need to cover in a pediatric practice?

At minimum, six message types layered on top of a household that can hold several children on different schedules: same-day sick-call triage that separates an urgent symptom report from a routine question before either waits behind the other; well-visit and immunization recall campaigns that run as a standing list, not a one-time reminder; school, camp, and sports-form requests that carry a deadline and should not sit in a general voicemail box; prescription refill requests routed to clinical staff rather than a shared inbox; appointment confirmations and reminders that account for more than one child on the same account; and after-hours routing that gets a parent's late-night message to the right next step, whether that is tomorrow's sick slot or tonight's on-call instructions. Most practices run each of these through a different tool, which is why the family that calls about a fever, mentions a camp form is due Friday, and has a sibling three months overdue for a well visit gets one of those three things handled and the other two lost.

Communications software is supposed to put every message type into one record per household, reachable on whichever channel that family actually answers. It only earns the name if it tells a fever message from a form request on sight, because treating both the same way is the exact failure a pediatric front desk cannot absorb during an RSV surge or the August school rush.

## Why does a reminder-only or single-channel tool fail pediatric practices the most?

Most communications platforms default to whichever channel was cheapest to build, usually a one-way text or an automated call, and stop at the next-visit reminder. That default is fine for confirming tomorrow's well visit. It fails on the message types where pediatric risk actually concentrates: a parent describing a symptom that needs a same-day slot or a nurse-line call, and a vaccine-recall list that nobody works because the one staff member who could is answering phones.

A reminder-only tool also cannot handle the household. One parent's phone carries two or three children at different points on the immunization and well-visit calendar, and a message about the 2-year-old that ignores the 6-year-old's overdue kindergarten visit is a missed opportunity, not just an incomplete one. The evidence above points the same direction: parents already want electronic communication and already tell platforms which channel they will actually answer, so the gap is not demand, it is a tool built to triage and to recognize a family rather than a single ticket.

## How to evaluate pediatric patient communications software before you buy: 5 steps

1. Confirm the system reads your practice-management schedule and the immunization forecast, and that it can tell a same-day symptom message from a routine one before either reaches staff.
2. Check that well-visit and immunization recall runs as a standing campaign across the whole patient panel, not a one-time reminder sent once and forgotten.
3. Verify the platform recognizes a household, not just a patient record, so a message about one child can surface a sibling's overdue visit.
4. Ask how school, camp, and sports-form requests get routed, and whether they carry a deadline instead of sitting in a general queue.
5. Confirm every channel is two-way and available after hours, since a parent's 9 PM portal message about a fever should not wait until morning to be triaged.

## What Should Still Go to a Human?

Any symptom question goes to a nurse or physician, never to a scheduling flow. A newborn call about fever, feeding, or jaundice goes straight to a nurse or the on-call physician regardless of how the message is worded. A parent declining a vaccine needs a conversation with the pediatrician, not another text. Billing disputes, a custody question about who may schedule for a child, and any clinical judgment call belong to staff. Software can triage the volume, flag it by urgency, and route it fast; it does not diagnose, and it does not decide what a symptom means.
