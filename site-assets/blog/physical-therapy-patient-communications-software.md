## The Data Behind the Decision

The clearest verified evidence on physical therapy no-shows comes from a 2021 retrospective analysis of scheduling data from January 2013 to April 2015, covering 6,971 patients scheduled for an initial outpatient physical therapist evaluation. Ten percent (698 patients) failed to show. The strongest scheduling-related predictor was wait time: each additional day between booking and the scheduled evaluation raised the odds of a no-show, and unemployment also raised the odds, while being female or being 40 or older lowered them (*Physical Therapy*, 2021, DOI 10.1093/ptj/pzab047). That finding matters past the first visit too, because every subsequent visit in a plan of care carries the same scheduling-gap risk the initial evaluation does.

Adherence between visits looks just as exposed. A February 2026 Ohio State Wexner Medical Center survey of 1,006 Americans found 76% did not complete all of their assigned at-home physical therapy exercises, with forgetting or lacking a reminder as the most common reason, followed by not having enough time. Adults 65 and older were more likely to finish everything (30%) than adults under 30 (12%). A plan of care that depends on exercises happening between visits, and nobody checking whether they did, is relying on patients to self-manage the exact behavior the survey found three in four of them do not complete.

## What does patient communications software actually need to cover in a physical therapy practice?

At minimum, six message types across a plan of care that runs for weeks, not a single visit: referral intake and initial-evaluation scheduling from the referring orthopedic surgeon, primary care physician, or other provider; appointment reminders and confirmations for every visit in the plan, not just the first one; home exercise program check-ins between sessions, so a missed exercise surfaces before the next visit instead of showing up as a surprise in the clinic; re-engagement outreach for a patient who skips a visit or goes quiet before finishing treatment; insurance authorization and visit-cap tracking, since most plans of care run against a capped number of approved visits; and a discharge or progress update sent back to the referring provider once treatment ends. Most practices run each of these through a different tool: a scheduling system for reminders, a front-desk phone call for referral intake, a printed sheet for home exercises. No single tool knows a patient missed their home program this week, is three visits into an eight-visit authorization, and has not rebooked.

Communications software is supposed to put every message type into one record per patient and per plan of care, reachable on whatever channel that patient actually answers. It only earns the name if it treats a mid-plan dropout differently from a routine reminder, because a patient who stops coming in week three has not finished treatment and is not coming back on their own.

## Why does a reminder-only or single-channel tool fail physical therapy practices the most?

Most communications platforms default to whichever channel was cheapest to build, usually a one-way text or an automated call, and stop at the next-visit reminder. That default is tolerable for confirming tomorrow's appointment. It fails on the message types where a multi-visit specialty's real risk sits: a home exercise program that goes out once and is never checked, and a patient who skips visit four of eight and never hears from the practice again until the authorization expires.

A reminder-only tool also cannot answer back. A patient who has a question about an exercise, or wants to know how many authorized visits remain, gets silence unless a staff member happens to see the message. That gap is exactly where the evidence above points: scheduling delay predicted no-shows at the very first visit, and lack of a reminder or accountability between visits was the top reason patients gave for skipping their home program, both signals a two-way system could catch and act on before the plan of care quietly stalls.

## How to evaluate physical therapy patient communications software before you buy: 5 steps

1. Confirm the system reads and writes to your practice management or EMR system, and that it can track a plan-of-care record across every visit, not just the next appointment.
2. Check that home exercise program completion gets its own tracked status between visits, separate from a routine appointment reminder, since adherence gaps are where mid-plan dropout risk concentrates.
3. Verify every channel is two-way, so a patient asking about an exercise or an authorized-visit count reaches a response, not a dead end.
4. Ask how the platform re-engages a patient who skips a visit mid-plan, and whether that outreach happens automatically or depends on staff noticing a gap.
5. Confirm insurance authorization and visit-cap tracking is built in, and how a discharge or progress summary gets documented back to the referring physician instead of a phone call nobody logs.

## What Should Still Go to a Human?

Any clinical judgment call: progressing an exercise program, assessing a patient's pain response, deciding when a patient is ready for discharge. Software can confirm a referral was received, check whether a home program was completed, and flag a dropout risk fast; the physical therapist still makes the clinical call. Anything a patient reports during outreach that sounds urgent, a sharp increase in pain, a new injury, a reaction to a new exercise, should route to clinical staff the same day, not sit in a queue. Insurance appeals and authorization disputes go to your billing team.
