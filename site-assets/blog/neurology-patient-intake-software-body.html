The Data Behind the Decision

The wait before the first visit is long enough to use, if intake starts at booking instead of in the waiting room. A study published in Neurology in 2026 found that commercially insured patients waited an average of 49.7 days for their first visit with a neurology provider, with a median wait of 25.0 days (Brain & Life / American Academy of Neurology, 2026). A separate study of Medicare participants found a median wait of 34 days from referral to the first neurologist visit, and 18% of patients waited more than 90 days (Wait Time to See a Neurologist After Referral Among Medicare Participants). Three to seven weeks is enough time to collect a seizure or headache history, have outside imaging and EEG results sent, and confirm insurance, if the practice starts that work the day the visit is booked rather than the day the patient arrives.

Insurance details drive the treatment start. 93% of physicians say prior authorization delays access to necessary care, and more than one in four report the process has led to a serious adverse event for a patient in their care (American Academy of Neurology, citing the 2024 AMA physician survey). In neurology the authorization for a disease-modifying therapy, a CGRP inhibitor, or a newer anti-seizure medication cannot begin until the plan details are correct, and a card photographed at intake is days gained on the start date. And 82% of patients try to book care outside a practice's regular office hours, which is also when they are most likely to sit down and complete an intake questionnaire on their phone.

What does neurology patient intake software actually do?

It starts the moment a visit is booked. The patient gets a text and email from the practice with a link to the questionnaire, matched to why they were referred: a seizure diary and witness-account prompts for epilepsy, a headache questionnaire with trigger and medication-trial history for migraine, a disability and symptom-progression history for MS, or a tremor and gait history for a movement-disorder referral. It asks the records question (which EEG, MRI, nerve conduction study, or lab panel, and where it was done), captures insurance and pharmacy details, reminds the patient what to have sent ahead, and answers questions about the forms in the same thread. On the day, arrival check-in confirms identity, insurance, and pharmacy, collects any outstanding signature, and captures that visit's baseline measure. The answers land in the chart as structured data the neurologist reads before walking in.

What separates that from a form portal is the conversation around it. A link sent once gets completed by the patients who were going to complete it anyway. Intake software that follows up, answers "which scan do you mean," and confirms the visit in the same thread gets completed by the rest.

How is intake different in neurology?

The history is the diagnosis. In most specialties intake collects demographics and a chief complaint. In neurology the questionnaire is clinical evidence: exactly what happens before, during, and after a seizure and what a witness saw, where a headache starts and what makes it worse, how long symptoms have progressed and in what pattern, and what happened on every prior AED, DMT, or triptan and why it stopped. Those answers let the neurologist decide in the first ten minutes which direction the workup goes, and patients give more complete, more accurate answers on their own phone, away from the counter, than they do rushed in an exam room.

The second difference is the outside record. The referring physician's or emergency department's imaging, EEG, and labs are usually already done before the referral, and the visit is far more productive when they are in the chart rather than reordered. Intake software does not fetch them; it gets the patient to say where they were done so the practice can request them before the visit, the same approach the orthopedic patient intake software guide describes for prior imaging in that specialty. The third difference is the measure. A seizure diary, a validated headache index, or a disability-progression history captured at intake becomes the baseline every later visit is compared to, which is also the message-layer work the neurology patient communications software guide covers in more depth.

How to run patient intake in a neurology practice: 6 steps

  1. Rebuild the questionnaire for the phone, by subspecialty. A seizure diary for epilepsy, a headache measure for migraine, a disability history for MS, a tremor and gait history for movement disorders, each with the records question and medication history built in. Physicians sign off once per template.
  2. Trigger it at booking. The link goes out the day the visit is scheduled, with a reply channel, not a week before the visit.
  3. Follow up in the thread. Unfinished questionnaires get a nudge, questions get an answer, and the visit gets confirmed in the same conversation.
  4. Start the records and insurance work early. The outside-records request and the eligibility check run from the intake answers, days before the visit.
  5. Run check-in on the patient's phone. Identity, insurance, pharmacy, signatures, and that visit's measure, with the desk handling only exceptions.
  6. Report weekly. Intake completion before the visit, measure completion at check-in, and desk time per arrival, shared with the front desk the way completion-rate improvement programs in other specialties have done.

What Should Still Go to a Human?

Reading the questionnaire and deciding what it means is the neurologist's job. Requesting and reconciling outside records and submitting authorizations belong to the practice's staff in the systems built for them. A patient who cannot complete the forms, who reports something urgent in the symptom section, such as a new seizure, a sudden severe headache, or a fall, or who disputes a balance at check-in, gets a person immediately. Intake software should surface those cases, not hide them in a completion percentage.

Frequently Asked Questions

What should a neurology new patient questionnaire include?
A subspecialty-matched history: seizure semiology and witness account for epilepsy, headache location, duration, triggers, and prior medication trials for migraine, symptom progression and prior relapse history for MS, a family history of neurologic disease, current and prior AEDs, DMTs, or triptans with the reason each was stopped, and a list of outside EEGs, imaging, and labs with where they were done. Sent before the visit and in the chart before the neurologist opens it.

Does intake software collect prior EEG, MRI, or EMG results?
Patient-facing intake software collects the patient's account of what was done and where, and reminds the patient to have records sent. Pulling the documents themselves is a records-request workflow the practice runs in the EHR. The value is that the patient answers before the visit, so the request goes out before the visit instead of after.

What does arrival check-in look like with intake software?
The patient confirms identity, insurance, and pharmacy on their phone, signs anything still outstanding, and completes that visit's baseline measure, whether that is a seizure count, a headache index, or a disability-progression check. The desk handles only exceptions: a new card, a failed form, a patient who needs help.

Can intake software start a prior authorization for a DMT, a CGRP inhibitor, or an AED?
Clinekt verifies insurance before the visit. Prior authorizations stay a billing workflow in the practice management system and the payer portal, and intake collects the card image and plan details early so that work can start days before the visit rather than at the counter.

How does intake connect to care between visits?
The same baseline measure captured at intake, a seizure diary, a headache index, or a disability-progression history, becomes the comparison point for every later visit. A platform that runs scheduled check-ins and collects the same measure between visits gives the neurologist a trend rather than a single snapshot.
