## The Data Behind the Decision

The clearest verified evidence on urology no-shows comes from a 2021 retrospective analysis published in <em>Urology Practice</em>, reviewing 83,983 appointments across 17 providers at a single urban academic practice from May 2017 to December 2020. Overall, 7,592 appointments, 9.0%, were missed. The rate varied sharply by why the patient was being seen: 4.5% for oncology visits, 9.6% for benign urology, and 13.0% for pediatric urology. Medicaid coverage was associated with the highest missed-visit rate at 17.2%, a prior no-show in the past year roughly doubled the odds of missing again, and a longer gap between scheduling and the visit date also raised the odds (<a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37145458/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">White et al., <em>Urology Practice</em>, 2021;8(4):480-486</a>).

The same analysis found telephone and video visits had missed-visit rates of roughly 2.9% and 2.8%, well below any in-person category. That gap does not mean urology should move visits to the phone. It means reach and follow-through drive attendance as much as the visit type does, which is the same lever a communications platform is built to pull: get the reminder, the instruction, or the recall message to the patient on a channel they will actually see, with enough lead time to act on it.

## What does patient communications software actually need to cover in a urology practice?

At minimum, six message types across a visit that often spans a referral, a procedure, and ongoing surveillance: new-consult intake and scheduling from a referring primary care physician flagging an elevated PSA, hematuria, or a stone episode; appointment reminders and confirmations for both consult and procedure visits; pre-procedure instructions such as anticoagulant hold times, bowel prep, or day-of consent for a cystoscopy, biopsy, or vasectomy; post-procedure follow-up that distinguishes a routine catheter-removal reminder from an urgent symptom report; PSA and stone surveillance recall, so a monitoring interval does not quietly lapse; and prior authorization and insurance tracking, since many urology procedures require authorization before they can be scheduled. Most practices run each of these through a different tool or a staff member's personal tracking system. No single system today knows a patient is three days post-procedure, due for a PSA recheck next month, and still waiting on a prior authorization for a second procedure.

Communications software is supposed to put every message type into one record per patient, reachable on whatever channel that patient actually answers. It only earns the name if it treats a reported post-procedure symptom differently from a routine reminder, because the two require different urgency and different staff, not the same template.

## Why does a reminder-only or single-channel tool fail urology practices the most?

Most communications platforms default to whichever channel was cheapest to build, usually a one-way text or an automated call, and stop at the next-visit reminder. That default is tolerable for confirming a routine follow-up. It fails on the message types where urology's real risk sits: a post-procedure message that could be a routine question or could be a patient who cannot urinate and needs same-day attention, and a PSA or stone surveillance interval that has no visit attached to trigger a reminder at all.

A reminder-only tool also cannot answer back. A patient asking whether a pre-op instruction applies to their specific medication, or reporting a fever three days after a procedure, gets silence unless a staff member happens to see the message in time. A prior authorization that stalls silently in a payer queue can push a scheduled procedure back weeks before anyone at the practice notices. Those are exactly the gaps a two-way, record-aware system is built to close.

## How to evaluate urology patient communications software before you buy: 5 steps

1. Confirm the system reads and writes to your practice management or EMR system, and that it can track a single patient record across referral, procedure, and surveillance, not just the next appointment.
2. Check that PSA and stone surveillance recall runs on its own tracked interval, independent of whether a visit is already on the calendar.
3. Verify every channel is two-way, so a patient reporting a post-procedure symptom or asking about a pre-op instruction reaches a response, not a dead end.
4. Ask how the platform distinguishes a routine message from an urgent one, and whether an urgent report routes to clinical staff the same day.
5. Confirm prior authorization and insurance tracking is built in, with a visible status so a stalled authorization surfaces before it delays a scheduled procedure.

## What Should Still Go to a Human?

Any clinical judgment call: interpreting a PSA result or biopsy finding, deciding how to manage a reported complication, and any post-procedure symptom that sounds urgent, inability to urinate, a high fever, or heavy bleeding, which should route to clinical staff the same day rather than sit in a queue. Software can confirm a referral was received, track whether a surveillance interval is due, and flag a reported symptom fast; the urologist still makes the clinical call. Prior authorization appeals and billing disputes go to your billing team.
