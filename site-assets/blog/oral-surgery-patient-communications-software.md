## The Data Behind the Decision

The cleanest verified evidence on surgical no-shows does not come from oral surgery itself, no peer-reviewed study specific to OMFS patient attendance turned up in a direct search, but from a closely analogous surgical specialty. A 2022 retrospective chart review of an academic otolaryngology practice, covering surgeries scheduled from 2006 to 2019, found an overall no-show rate of 3.63% (1,752 no-shows against 46,440 completed surgeries). Multivariate analysis found decreasing age, a planned outpatient case, head and neck oncology subspecialty, increasing distance from the facility, a higher prior number of clinic no-shows, and not having private insurance all statistically significant predictors of not showing (Trott et al., *Laryngoscope*, 132(9), 2022). Oral surgery shares the same referral-in, procedure-scheduled structure as this otolaryngology population, which is why it is the most relevant verified data point available, read as a directional surgical-specialty benchmark rather than an OMFS-specific figure.

Investment has not caught up to where the risk concentrates. An October 2024 poll of 302 medical groups found 59% were prioritizing AI investment in clinical documentation and scribing, versus only 10% in patient communication (MGMA Stat, 2024). An oral surgery practice runs several distinct communication threads per patient, referral intake, pre-op sedation compliance, post-op recovery, and a referral-loop update back to the referring dentist or orthodontist, and most of that volume still runs through whatever reminder feature shipped with the practice management system, or a phone call nobody logs.

## What does patient communications software actually need to cover in an oral surgery practice?

At minimum, six message types: referral intake and consult scheduling from a general dentist, orthodontist, periodontist, or endodontist; appointment reminders and confirmations for the consult and the procedure itself; pre-operative sedation and anesthesia instructions, fasting windows, medication holds, and a confirmed ride home, that a patient must actually follow, not just receive; post-operative recovery guidance for extractions, implants, and wisdom teeth; insurance verification across the medical-versus-dental split that OMFS billing regularly has to navigate; and a referral-loop update back to the referring practice once treatment is complete. Most practices run each of these through a different tool: a scheduling system for reminders, a front-desk phone call for referral intake, a printed sheet for pre-op instructions. No single tool knows a patient was referred in for an impacted wisdom tooth consult, confirmed sedation compliance, and still needs a completion update sent back to the referring dentist.

Communications software is supposed to put every message type into one record per patient and per referral, reachable on whatever channel that patient, or that referring office, actually answers. It only earns the name if it treats a pre-op compliance check differently from a routine reminder, because a missed sedation instruction is a same-day cancellation risk, not just an inconvenience.

## Why does a reminder-only or single-channel tool fail oral surgery practices the most?

Most communications platforms default to whichever channel was cheapest to build, usually a one-way text or an automated call, and stop at the reminder. That default is tolerable for confirming tomorrow's consult. It fails on the message types where a surgical specialty's real risk sits: a referral request that arrives by fax or phone and never gets acknowledged back to the referring office, and a pre-op instruction that goes out once and is never confirmed as understood before the patient arrives having eaten breakfast on a day they were sedated.

A reminder-only tool also cannot answer back. A patient who replies to a pre-op text asking whether a specific medication counts against the fasting window, or a referring office that wants to know if their patient showed up, gets silence unless a staff member happens to see the message. That gap is exactly where the otolaryngology data above points: a planned outpatient case and distance from the facility were both significant predictors of not showing, both signals a two-way system could catch and act on before the appointment, not after.

## How to evaluate oral surgery patient communications software before you buy: 5 steps

1. Confirm the system reads and writes to your practice management system rather than working from a stale export, and that it can track a referral record alongside a patient record.
2. Check that pre-op sedation and anesthesia compliance gets its own confirmed-received status, separate from a routine appointment reminder, since a missed instruction here is a same-day cancellation risk.
3. Verify every channel is two-way, for both the patient and the referring office, not just the patient.
4. Ask how the platform follows up with a patient who ignores a pre-op instruction, and whether that follow-up happens automatically or depends on staff remembering to check.
5. Confirm consent, opt-in, and HIPAA handling across phone, text, and portal, and how a completed-treatment update gets documented back to the referring dentist or orthodontist instead of a phone call nobody logs.

## What Should Still Go to a Human?

Any clinical judgment call, assessing anesthesia risk, deciding a treatment plan, explaining a surgical finding to a patient or a referring provider. Software can confirm a referral was received, get a pre-op instruction in front of a patient, and flag a status change fast; the oral surgeon still makes the clinical call. Anything a patient reports during outreach that sounds urgent, uncontrolled bleeding, a fever, swelling that is getting worse, should route to clinical staff the same day, not sit in a queue waiting for a scheduled check. Insurance appeals and financial hardship requests go to your billing team.
